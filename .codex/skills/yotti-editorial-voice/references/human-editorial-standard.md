# Human editorial standard

## Three kinds of prose

### Mechanical prose

Mechanical prose fills a predefined structure. Paragraphs have similar length, every section performs the same function, lists appear because the template expects them, and transitions announce what comes next. The text may be correct but feels assembled.

### Recognisably GPT-like prose

GPT-like prose is smoother and more ambitious, yet still predictable. It often:

- opens with a staged cinematic scene regardless of the subject;
- relies on symmetrical contrasts such as “not X, but Y”;
- turns ordinary advice into a metaphor, lesson, or manifesto;
- gives every paragraph a polished concluding sentence;
- uses abstract phrases such as “change the scale”, “the city reveals itself”, “a chapter rather than a destination”, or “the real journey begins”;
- addresses the reader with a sequence of soft commands;
- repeats universal blocks such as key takeaways, conclusion, and FAQ even when they add nothing;
- sounds complete and agreeable but lacks the selective judgement of an editor.

These signals do not prove who wrote a text. They are editorial defects in their own right and must be removed.

### Human journalistic prose

Human prose selects rather than exhausts. It uses the rhythm the material needs: a short factual sentence may sit beside a longer explanation; one section may be brief and another substantial. Transitions follow the subject, not a formula. Concrete names, actions, limitations, and useful observations do most of the work. The writer can be vivid without making every sentence quotable.

Human editing also leaves some sentences plain. “За Сокровищницей маршрут продолжается к театру и Царским гробницам” is better service journalism than a metaphor about doors, chapters, or final points.

## Decision spine

Before drafting a substantial guide, write one sentence that states who the
piece is for, what decision it helps make, and the main trade-off. Every major
section must then do a different job: make a choice, support it, describe a
route leg, name a limitation, or provide a fallback. Delete sections that only
repeat the destination mood or restate the lead.

For a route guide, alternate places with movement and decisions. Do not write a
sequence of identical city portraits. State why each stop is a base, what the
reader does there, and what changes on the next leg.

## Titles

Write a title as a real editor would name the piece to a colleague. Prefer a recognisable question, route, place, or decision. Search wording may appear naturally, but never force an awkward noun chain.

Reject:

- “Петра после первого кадра: зачем оставить время за знаменитым фасадом”;
- “Петра, кроме Сокровищницы”;
- “У Сокровищницы: сделать паузу, но не ставить точку”;
- “Petra after the first photograph: why the city deserves more than its famous facade”.

Prefer:

- “Петра: что посмотреть после Сокровищницы”;
- “Что посмотреть в Петре: маршрут от Сика до Царских гробниц”;
- “Petra beyond the Treasury: a guide to the ancient city”.

Read the title aloud. If it needs explanation, sounds translated, or tries to impress before it informs, rewrite it.

## Sentence and paragraph choices

Replace a general claim with the fact it is hiding.

- Weak: “Тогда знаменитый первый вид становится не финалом, а дверью.”
- Better: “За Сокровищницей находятся театр, Царские гробницы и бывший центр города.”

- Weak: “А затем совершите самое важное действие дня — продолжите путь.”
- Better: “После Сокровищницы маршрут идёт дальше по широкой долине.”

- Weak: “This is the small decision that separates a visit to a landmark from a day inside an ancient city.”
- Better: “Beyond the Treasury, the path continues towards the theatre, Royal Tombs and civic centre.”

Do not replace every weak line with a blander synonym. Recover the useful fact, observation, or decision underneath it.

## Headings and SEO blocks

Use headings as navigation. “Что находится за Сокровищницей” is useful; “сделать паузу, но не ставить точку” performs a mood.

Key takeaways help comparison and complex instructions. FAQ helps when real follow-up questions remain. Neither is mandatory. Do not repeat the article in a conclusion merely to satisfy a template. A closing paragraph may finish the final section naturally.

SEO value comes from answering the query clearly, naming relevant places, supporting facts, and satisfying the reader. Repeating a query or imposing the same SERP-shaped layout on every article reduces editorial quality.

## Visual writing

An information visual must answer a question that prose alone answers less
quickly. A route map shows order and geography; a table shows stops, nights and
connections; a timeline shows sequence. The surrounding text must still carry
the complete answer.

Write alt text for what is visibly important. Write a caption for the editorial
observation the reader should take from the image. Do not make the caption a
second alt, a slogan, or a generic travel compliment.

Animated maps require a static or text fallback, an accessible name and reduced
motion. Until the Yotti importer is verified, treat animation as a pilot rather
than a default promise.

## Final read

During the separate editorial pass:

1. Read only the title and headings. Rewrite anything vague, ornate, or interchangeable with another country.
2. Read the first two paragraphs aloud. Remove stage directions, inflated suspense, and claims that do not lead to useful information.
3. Circle every contrast, metaphor, imperative, and summary sentence. Keep only those that earn their place.
4. Check that adjacent paragraphs do not have the same length and rhetorical shape by habit.
5. Verify that the final paragraph ends the subject rather than delivering a motivational lesson.
6. Run the deterministic style check. Passing it is necessary but not sufficient; editorial judgement remains responsible for the result.

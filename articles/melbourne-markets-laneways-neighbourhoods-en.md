A useful Melbourne day moves through three scales: Queen Victoria Market in the morning, a short set of central laneways after lunch, then one neighbourhood for the evening. Choose Fitzroy for active streets and small shops; choose Carlton for terraces, cafés and a quieter finish. Trying to cover both usually turns the final hours into transport.

This structure prevents the city becoming a collection of murals and coffee stops. The market shows ordinary shopping, the centre reveals dense passageways, and the tram carries you into streets where people are finishing work and returning home.

## Start with a working market

Queen Victoria Market is most useful as a window into the city’s morning rather than a list of compulsory foods. Walk through the produce halls, stop at bread, cheese or seasonal fruit and choose a simple breakfast. The official listing carries current opening days, so check it before making the market the anchor of your route.

Do not sample continuously. Eat one thing and carry another only if it will survive the walk. The market then remains a beginning rather than an oversized breakfast that slows the rest of the day.

Notice the shift between food traders and general merchandise. One side serves weekly routines; the other brings makers, visitors and practical shopping into the same sheds. That distinction is more informative than a broad claim about “market culture”.

## Use laneways as paths, not trophies

Move from the market towards the central grid and choose a few arcades and lanes that form a sensible line. Hosier Lane often becomes the single target, but Melbourne has no final version of its street art. Murals change, businesses turn over and a quiet service lane nearby may tell a better city story.

Build a short route between two major streets instead of a zigzag through ten pins. Look upward in an arcade, then notice how a lane is being used today. If one wall is surrounded by tour groups, continue walking; the quality of the day does not depend on photographing it.

Choose lunch according to appetite, not the longest social-media queue. A central wait can consume the time you meant to spend in a neighbourhood. A small meal or later coffee keeps room for dinner where the city becomes more residential.

## Let a tram change the scale

Take one direct tram away from the centre. During the ride, towers give way to lower façades, office crowds mix with students and cyclists, and small businesses replace central arcades. That transition is part of the itinerary.

Check live routing and payment guidance through the official transport service. A line number does not belong in an evergreen guide when construction and service changes can make it stale.

## Choose Fitzroy or Carlton

Pick Fitzroy when you want active streets, small retailers, galleries and bars. One main street and two side streets are enough. The point is to see how the district fits together, not to prove you reached every recommended door.

Carlton offers a softer landing. Walk between terrace houses, stop at a bakery or bookshop and end with dinner without inventing a second evening route.

Neither neighbourhood needs to represent all of Melbourne. Its job is to show houses, small gardens, bicycles at railings and places people visit without a guidebook after the central grid has receded.

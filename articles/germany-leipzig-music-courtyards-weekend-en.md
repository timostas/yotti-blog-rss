Leipzig fits a weekend well, provided you do not try to follow its musical history in chronological order. The old town is compact, yet each church, museum and passage can take longer than the map suggests. A workable visit uses short clusters: one musical story, two neighbouring courtyards, time for coffee and a separate half-day in one lived-in district.

Leipzig's official tourism site calls it a city of music and connects more than 500 composers with the city. Its Music Trail links 23 sites over roughly 5.3 kilometres. That is a helpful frame, not an assignment to complete every stop. For two days, choose two figures or institutions that create a meaningful conversation.

## Saturday: music within a few streets

Begin at St Thomas Church. Its association with Bach matters, but the square is also a useful point from which to understand how close the central streets are. If you plan to enter the church, visit a museum or attend a performance, give one of those experiences proper time. Briefly sampling all three rarely adds more than choosing one carefully.

Next, choose one composer's house. The Mendelssohn House offers a domestic scale: rooms, objects and the sense of a working private life rather than an overview of centuries. The Bach Museum is the simpler alternative if you want to stay near the church. Do not schedule both simply because they are close; the detail of the first can blur during the second.

Treat a concert as a scheduled opportunity, not a guaranteed attraction. Programmes change, some events require early booking and a church service is not a tourist performance. Check the official calendar, admission conditions and nature of the event. Without a suitable performance, the musical walk still works through buildings, distances and the city's acoustic spaces.

## Passages are a trading system, not a mall

Leipzig's passages and trade-fair courtyards grew from the city's commercial role. Many connect separate streets, change the light and reveal an inner courtyard unexpectedly. Walk them as an urban route: enter from one street, notice how the building handles movement and emerge in another part of the block.

Choose two contrasting spaces. Mädler Passage supplies the ceremonial side, while a less famous neighbouring courtyard may be quieter and more austere. Pause for coffee between them. The value lies not in accumulating names but in recognising that the old centre is built from permeable inner routes as well as façades.

## Sunday: one more musical note and one district

Approach music from another angle in the morning. After a Bach-focused Saturday, choose Mendelssohn, Schumann or a contemporary venue. If you already visited a composer's home, simply follow a few Music Trail markers. Repetition is useful only when the scale changes from institution to street.

After lunch, travel to one neighbourhood. Plagwitz offers a walk along the canal, former industrial buildings and independent spaces. Südvorstadt gives a more linear route along an active street with cafés. A short visit does not need both; connecting them turns transport into the organiser of your afternoon.

Choose a café or early dinner along the route rather than turning it into a distant destination. Recheck opening hours on the day; Sunday trading patterns and private events can alter the plan. If a place is closed, another block on foot is a better substitute than an urgent trip across town.

## Book the clock, leave the streets flexible

Reserve only the experience attached to a specific time: a concert, guided visit or museum slot where required. Leave passages, courtyards and the neighbourhood walk movable. Rain can be spent under a glass roof, while a clear hour belongs beside the canal.

A Leipzig weekend is not a complete course in music history. It is a conversation between church and sitting room, grand passage and modest courtyard, old centre and former industrial district. With one real choice in each block, the city has time to be heard without becoming a cultural endurance test.

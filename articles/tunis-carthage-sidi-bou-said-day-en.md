The most workable order is Tunis medina in the morning, one deliberately chosen section of Carthage after lunch, then Sidi Bou Said for the late afternoon and evening. Each stop asks for a different pace. Trying to cover the entire archaeological zone as well will leave most of the day inside taxis or trains.

The TGM suburban railway links Tunis with Carthage, Sidi Bou Said and La Marsa. Check its live operation before setting out. Within Carthage, the remains are spread through a modern residential area, so a taxi can be useful even when the longer journey follows the railway.

## Give the medina a single direction

Enter the medina with one landmark in mind and let the smaller observations fill the route. Workshops, deliveries, small courtyards and cafés all share the narrow streets. A rigid list of shops quickly becomes frustrating because the district is doing its ordinary work around you.

Choose one substantial food stop before leaving central Tunis. Mechouia salad, a fish dish or Tunisian tajine can anchor the meal in the cooking of the capital. A clear menu and a steady local lunch trade are more useful signals than a long online ranking.

Leave before the afternoon is far advanced. Carthage is not a compact ruin with one entrance and a short circular path.

## Choose the Roman baths or the Punic ports

For a first visit, the Antonine Baths provide an immediate sense of Roman scale and a striking position beside the sea. The Punic ports offer a quieter way to consider Carthage as a maritime city. Either can be the main archaeological stop; adding one smaller nearby site is usually enough.

This choice prevents transport from consuming the visit. Travellers most interested in monumental architecture can spend longer at the baths. Those drawn to the city’s seafaring history may prefer the ports and the surrounding landscape. Current access and opening arrangements need checking on the day.

The official tourism site notes how the ancient remains are distributed across a broad area of present-day Carthage. If you use a taxi, agree on the named sites before starting rather than asking for a generic tour with an uncertain finish.

## Let Sidi Bou Said slow the afternoon

Sidi Bou Said is close enough to make a natural final stop, but its steep lanes deserve more than a quick photograph. The familiar palette of white walls and blue doors becomes more interesting away from the busiest central path, where ironwork, tiled details and ordinary entrances vary from house to house.

Pause for bambalouni, the warm ring-shaped doughnut associated with the village, or mint tea with pine nuts. One drink with a view of the bay makes more sense here than another sequence of marked sights.

Decide before walking downhill whether you still plan to eat in Tunis. The sea view may encourage a longer stop, and the climb back takes time. A late return is perfectly reasonable when the evening meal is moved to Sidi Bou Said or nearby La Marsa.

## Keep three transport anchors

Save the exit point from the medina, the exact Carthage site and the station or meeting address in Sidi Bou Said. If the TGM is disrupted, travel directly to the next anchor rather than attempting to preserve every minor stop. The day still works when its three main parts remain intact.
